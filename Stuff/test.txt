# Tyson and the Park Across the Street

Every morning, before the sun had fully climbed over the rooftops, Leo would hear the soft thump of paws on the wooden floor. Tyson was already awake—tail wagging, brown eyes bright, ready for the day.

They lived in a small house with white walls and a blue front door, directly across from Greenfield Park. From Leo’s bedroom window he could see the swings, the wide grassy field, and the old oak tree where birds gathered in the afternoon. It was close enough that Tyson would sit by the glass door and whine gently until Leo laughed and said, “All right, boy. Let’s go.”

Tyson was a boxer—solid, gentle, and a little clumsy when he got excited. His chest was white, his coat a warm fawn, and when he ran, his ears bounced like they had their own rhythm. Leo was eight, small for his age but quick on his feet, and Tyson had been his friend since the day the puppy had curled up on his lap and fallen asleep.

Their routine never changed, and neither of them wanted it to.

After breakfast—Leo’s toast, Tyson’s bowl—they crossed the quiet street. The park smelled of cut grass and damp earth. Leo threw the red ball as far as he could; Tyson would chase it, miss it once, spin in a circle, then scoop it up and bring it back, dropping it at Leo’s feet with a proud snort.

They played tag around the oak tree. They lay on the grass and watched clouds. Sometimes other children came with their dogs, and Tyson would bow his front legs and invite them to run. Leo never minded sharing his best friend. Tyson had enough joy for everyone.

In the evenings they walked home as the lamps in the park flickered on. Leo’s hand rested on Tyson’s broad head; Tyson’s leash hung loose between them. Back at the little house, Tyson would drink noisily from his bowl, then settle on the rug while Leo read or drew pictures of dogs that looked a lot like him.

At night, from the room across from the park, Leo could still hear distant laughter from the swings and the rustle of leaves in the wind. Tyson would sigh—a deep, contented boxer sigh—and press his warm side against the bed.

Leo would whisper, “See you in the morning, Tyson.”

And in the morning, the paws would thump again, the tail would wag, and they would cross the street to the park, where every day felt like the best one yet.
